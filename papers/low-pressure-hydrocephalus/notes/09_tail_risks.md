# Tail risks of sub-zero drainage and their clinical management

Every adverse event or failure mode reported in the corpus for patients
managed for low-/negative-pressure hydrocephalus (LPH/NegPH), source by
source, with what the authors did about it. Quotes are verbatim; FT = full
text read (OA XML or publisher PDF supplied by the user), abs = abstract only.
Frequencies are quoted only where a denominator is given. This is a literature
synthesis, not treatment advice.

## A. Per source

### Wu 2019 — FT (39 pts, 31 LPH / 8 NegPH; the only series with outcome statistics)
- Infection: "Eighteen cases were complicated by intracranial infections, which
  accounted for 46.15% of all 39 cases … The infection rate in NegPH patients
  was 87.50% and 35.48% in LPH patients. A significant difference in the
  infection rate was found" (P = 0.015). Organism note: "Pandrug resistant
  Acinetobacter" in one; "spore bacteria in the CSF after VP shunt".
- Death: "Eight patients … died, with a mortality rate of 20.51%. Among them, 6
  patients died from intracranial infections, which accounted for 75.0% and
  were the main reason for in-hospital death in this group." "62.5% of NegPH
  patients died, which is significantly higher than the LPH patients (5/8 vs.
  3/29; P = 0.0056)."
- Function: "At 3-month follow-up, the average GOS-E score was 2.73 (95% CI
  2.34–3.12) … 1.63 (95% CI 0.86–2.39) in NegPH patients and 3.03 in LPH
  patients". Study-level statement: "all patients had a poor prognosis".
- Mechanism offered: "it was very difficult to drain CSF externally under
  low/negative ventricular pressure, which was very easy to backflow and cause
  CSF infection in the brain."
- Management: "Preventing backflow, strict aseptic manipulation, and reducing
  the drainage time were the key issues to prevent CNS infection during CSF
  external drainage." Positional/venous adjuncts: "Neck wrapping, bellyband,
  horizontal head position, and even the Trendelenburg position can be used to
  improve ventricular pressure." Shunt preconditions after infection: normal
  temperature, normal leukocyte/neutrophil proportion, "CSF tests in all
  ventricles and the arachnoid space should be normal. If one ventricle is
  divided into a plurality of chambers after ventriculitis, it should be ensured
  that the CSF in those interior chambers is also normal. Finally, pneumocephalus
  should not be present." Device choice: "No anti-siphon programmable tube
  should be selected for low/negative hydrocephalus patients to prevent
  inadequate CSF drainage." "We do not recommend a lumboperitoneal shunt for
  patients with craniocervical cistern that is blurry or disappeared on CT."
  Salvage after shunt: "pump pressing should be tried. Several hundred to
  several thousand times a day VP pumps were pressed according to the ventricle
  size in CT scan and symptom recovery" (case 5: 600/day, "emergence from
  minimally conscious state").

### Akins 2011 — FT (9 pts)
- Autonomic/cardiac events at presentation: "Autonomic instability (brain
  storming) including hypertension, tachypnea, and fever was noted in two
  patients. Supraventricular tachyarrhythmias occurred in three patients with
  disproportionately dilated fourth ventricles. Symptoms resolved following LPH
  diagnosis and sub-zero treatment."
- Relapse: "Two patients (Cases 2 and 6) suffered relapse of LPH, but responded
  to sub-zero treatment. These patients restabilized with more aggressive CSF
  drainage using either a valveless ventriculoperitoneal shunt or a
  ventriculopleural shunt." Table: one patient "three relapses".
- Failure to wean: "Three patients had refractory LPH and were unable to
  tolerate positive pressure EVD drainage. A trial of cervical tourniquet in
  one patient was unsuccessful. For these three refractory LPH patients, two did
  well with ventriculopleural shunting and the third patient was managed with a
  valveless ventriculoperitoneal shunt."
- Over-drainage/infection with low-pressure shunts: "No long-term complications
  were observed with low pressure shunting such as shunt infection or subdural
  hematoma" (follow-up 6 mo–4 yr). Literature review: "We did not find any
  reported complications of treated LPH patients, such as slit ventricle
  syndrome or SDH formation."
- Deaths: none reported in the 9.

### Foster 2016 — FT (16 pts)
- Death: "1 patient died 2 months after ETV with an EVD still at a
  subatmospheric pressure" (one of the two ETV patients who were not shunted at
  the time of ETV). Cause not stated.
- Infection: illustrated case — "CSF analysis at the time of shunt
  externalization revealed an Enterobacter infection and the patient completed
  a 3 weeks course of meropenem with resolution of his CSF infection."
- Procedural: "There were no procedure-related complications from ETV or shunt
  placement." Shunts placed at ETV: "have not demonstrated shunt failure to
  date."
- Autonomic: "No patients presented with autonomic instability or cardiac
  arrhythmias, as has been reported in other LPH series" (contrast Akins).
- Presenting signs included "seizure".
- Duration as the risk: mean EVD 73 d (14–257) without ETV vs 114 d with ETV
  (P = 0.16; selection of refractory patients for ETV). Literature cited: "a
  patient who died following ETV without resolution of LPH".

### Godoy Hurtado 2023 — FT (6 pts)
- Over-drainage after low-pressure shunting: "Opening pressure reprogramming
  was needed to 4 mmHg due to asymptomatic subdural collections"; "marked
  radiological signs of hyper drainage with bilateral subdural collections
  (Figure 2f) without any clinical repercussions" — managed by raising the
  antigravitational unit "to the maximum allowed (40 mmHg), showing a
  progressive reduction"; another patient at 10 months "a CT scan showed
  complete ventricular collapse, requiring a rise to 4 mmHg."
- Early relapse after shunting: "On day 40 after the onset, he could receive a
  new shunt … 7 days later the patient presented low level of consciousness
  again with a new ventricular dilatation. At this moment it was decided to
  place a concomitant catheter in contralateral ventricle and start again
  negative drainage."
- Shunt infection: "In the context of fever and obtundation, infection of the
  shunt system (programmable) was diagnosed 4 months later. VPS was
  externalized and internalized once the infection resolved." Later "After
  resolving CSF infections, in another center, he finally received a
  programmable ventricular atrial shunt … programmed in the range of 9 mmHg."
- Seizures: one patient "had generalized epileptic seizures that were difficult
  to control."
- Death: "Eventually, the patient died, three years after diagnosis" (tumour
  patient; cause not attributed to LPH).
- Presentation risks: "digestive symptoms consisting of nausea and vomiting in
  half of the cases, as well as other symptoms not classically described, such
  as bradycardia." Untreated: "the absence of treatment also implies a risk of
  death, as well as severe neurological damage that may be permanent."

### Salam 2025 — FT (1 refractory case, 111 hospital days)
- Background burden: "septic shock, pulmonary embolism, respiratory failure
  requiring tracheostomy, and percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy" (earlier
  admission); the index episode began with "an infected VPS" and "Enterococcus
  faecalis" ventriculitis, treated with removal of the shunt, EVD, and
  "intraventricular tissue plasminogen activator" for IVH.
- Pneumocephalus with re-enlargement: "repeat head CT demonstrated further
  enlargement of his ventricles and bifrontal pneumocephalus."
- Adjunct toxicity: after theophylline, "complete collapse of the ventricles to
  an almost slit-like appearance" within 4 h, then "focal seizures … with
  progression to status epilepticus. He was intubated for airway protection,
  started on a dexmedetomidine drip, and placed on continuous EEG monitoring …
  Status epilepticus resolved in 5 days". Authors: "Although he did not have a
  history of seizures, his recent severe traumatic brain injury placed him at
  high risk for epilepsy"; theophylline adverse effects listed as
  "tachycardia, restlessness, tremor, nausea, vomiting, and seizures."
- Outcome: "Over the next 45 days, he made progressive recovery and serial CT
  studies demonstrated persistently small, slit-like ventricles." Literature
  cited: after ventriculothecal shunting a patient "developed bilateral hygromas
  with symptoms suggestive of intracranial hypotension".

### Kuppler 2025 — FT (1 chronic NegPH case, 180 d EVD)
- Infection despite prophylactic exchanges: "A series of EVD changes was
  performed at regular intervals of 4 weeks for the purpose of infection
  prophylaxis. However, as a consequence of prolonged EVD treatment, the patient
  developed meningitis and ventriculitis due to a catheter-associated infection
  with Staphylococcus epidermidis."
- Systemic complications of the ICU stay: "hospital-acquired pneumonia and
  cholecystectomy following an episode of acute acalculous cholecystitis";
  "periods of both hypo- and hyperglycemic states, which were in turn
  accompanied by clinical deterioration."
- Over-drainage during a shunt trial: "After a short monitoring period,
  overdrainage was observed on follow-up cranial CT".
- Misattribution risk: "VPS dysfunction in LPH or NegPH can be mistaken as a
  cause of persistent ventriculomegaly and poor clinical status … to prevent
  unnecessary interventions and treatment complications."
- Management stance: "It is therefore imperative to accept the failure of
  common treatment after a certain point and consider alternative options to
  ensure outpatient care and reduce the risk of treatment complications";
  intermittent reservoir pumping "was found to be an effective method for
  stabilizing ventricle size without the need for further external ventricular
  drainage, which carries a risk of infection." "Short intervals between
  outpatient visits are recommended".

### Chen 2025 — FT (paediatric, 44 literature + 4 institutional)
- "Out of the 40 patients with a definitive outcome reported in the
  literature, 31 patients (75%) recovered to baseline … 3 patients remained
  with severe symptoms (GOS <3), and 6 patients (13%) eventually died." "15% of
  patients die from L&NPH or primary disease progression, a slightly higher
  mortality rate than in adults."
- Institutional: "our approach ultimately resulted in significant symptomatic
  improvement in three patients, in increase the risk of intracranial
  infection, and one patient ultimately died from intracranial infection."
- Duration cost: "due to the prolonged drainage time, further rehabilitation of
  the patient is delayed, affecting the patient's long-term quality of life."
- Paediatric-specific signs: "bradycardia, seizure, and failure to thrive".

### Wang 2024 — FT (1 case, aSAH)
- Over-drainage during sub-zero EVD: "NCCT performed on PBD 3 demonstrated
  small bilateral subdural hygromas … These hygromas were attributed to
  excessive CSF drainage" → EVD raised, with qEEG delta power as an early
  warning of re-enlargement.

### Doshi 2022 — FT (1 case)
- Over-drainage after shunting: "gross bilateral subdural hygroma caused by
  shunt over drainage at the valve pressure of 110 mmH2O which was treated by
  increasing shunt pressure to 180 mmH2O and surgical evacuation of hygroma";
  later a ventriculopleural shunt with "an antisiphon device (to prevent
  position related overdrainage)". Note the direct contradiction with Wu's
  advice against anti-siphon devices.
- Raised intra-abdominal pressure (18 mmHg) as a cause of recurrent VP shunt
  failure in LPH: "loss of differential pressure resulting in recurrent shunt
  dysfunction."

### Bruk 2026 — FT (2 cases, cervical collar)
- Risks named for drainage: "Drainage of any kind is highly invasive and risks
  infection, bleeding, misplacement, and catheter blockage"; lumbar drain
  "carry a risk of hemorrhagic and other procedural complications" — both
  patients were on dual antiplatelet therapy for flow-diverter stents, which
  "precluded such surgical intervention".
- Collar surveillance list: "discomfort, skin breakdown, dysphagia, dyspnea,
  facial or conjunctival venous congestion, and worsening headache"; "Neither
  patient in this series experienced such complications"; "a cloth barrier to
  protect the skin".
- Relapse on withdrawal: "follow-up CT demonstrated interval recurrent
  ventriculomegaly, temporally associated with collar discontinuation during
  rehabilitation; the collar was reapplied". Cited Hamilton & Price: "withdrawal
  after three days produced a decreased level of consciousness and recurrent
  ventriculomegaly".

### Abstract-only sources
- Pang 1994 (abs): "Seven patients failed to improve with multiple shunt
  revisions, including the use of low-pressure valves" — the risk of repeated
  futile revision before the diagnosis is made.
- Lesniak 2002 (abs, 10 pts): "At 1 year, eight patients (80%) showed good
  recovery to minimal disability; seven patients (70%) had resolving
  ventriculomegaly."
- Clarke 2006 (abs, 2 pts): "outcomes in both patients ultimately were poor."
- Keough 2021 (abs, 195 pts): "the mortality rate was 11%, 83% of pediatric and
  49% of adult patients returned to their pre-ALPH neurological functional
  status … Outcomes were related to both the severity of the underlying
  neurosurgical disease causing the hydrocephalus and the efficacy of ALPH
  treatment."
- Casado Pellejero 2022 (abs, 5 pts): "a high morbidity and mortality"; "One
  patient died after systemic infection (20%), 80% of the patients experienced
  good outcome"; "early identification … is essential to reduce
  complications".
- Hamilton & Price 2012 (abs, 20 pts): ETV "producing a rapid return of normal
  ICP dynamics and a significant decrease in the number of shunt-dependent
  patients."
- Bakmeedeniya 2026 (abs, systematic review): "delayed intervention, infection,
  and irreversible white-matter injury predicted poor recovery."
- Filippidis 2011 (abs): "recurrent episodes of shunt failure because the
  intracranial pressure is lower than the opening pressure of the valve."
- Diaz-Romero Paz 2019 (abs): the precipitating event was over-drainage of a
  cyst drain — "after overdrainage of the external cyst drain, the patient
  suffered neurologic deterioration."

## B. Synthesis: the tail risks and what the sources did

### Bottom line
1. The dominant tail risk is infection of a drain kept sub-zero for weeks to
   months, and it is concentrated in the negative-pressure subgroup (Wu: 87.5%
   infected, 62.5% dead). Every author who addresses it treats *drainage
   duration* as the modifiable variable — hence the push for early ETV
   (Hamilton & Price, Casado, Foster), reservoir pumping (Kuppler) and Wu's
   "reducing the drainage time"; scheduled catheter exchange alone did not
   prevent it (Kuppler).
2. The second cluster is the opposite failure once the brain re-stiffens:
   subdural collections and ventricular collapse under zero-pressure or
   valveless shunts (Godoy, Doshi, Kuppler) and under theophylline (Salam). The
   sources disagree on anti-siphon devices (Wu against, Doshi for), and Akins
   reports no SDH in 9 low-pressure shunts over years — so the risk is real but
   inconsistently observed.
3. Relapse is common enough that all series that followed patients report it
   (Akins 2/9; Godoy day 47; Bruk on collar withdrawal) and every relapse was
   managed by returning to sub-zero drainage, then a lower-resistance shunt —
   not by abandoning the diagnosis.
4. Mortality ranges from 0/9 (Akins) to 62.5% (Wu's NegPH), tracking infection
   and the underlying disease rather than the drainage method itself; no death
   in the corpus is attributed to sub-zero drainage per se, though Foster's
   post-ETV death occurred while still sub-zero.
5. Autonomic storming, SVT and bradycardia are presentation-phase risks that
   resolved with drainage in the only series reporting them (Akins), and were
   absent in Foster's series.
