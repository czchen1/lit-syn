# History and nomenclature

## 1. "Low-pressure hydrocephalus" as a synonym for NPH (1965–c.1990)

After Adams, Hakim and colleagues described normal-pressure hydrocephalus in
1965, many European authors preferred the term *low-pressure hydrocephalus* for
the same syndrome — an elderly patient with gait apraxia, dementia and
incontinence, communicating ventriculomegaly and a lumbar opening pressure in
the normal range. The 28 papers on the `historical_lph_as_nph` axis are of this
kind: cerebral blood flow before and after shunting (Greitz 1969, PMID 5359210;
Meixensberger 1989, PMID 2608784), isotope cisternography (Jensen 1970, PMID
5457870; Kulczycki 1978, PMID 683432), overnight pressure recordings that showed
B-waves and plateau waves in "so-called low-pressure hydrocephalus" (Symon 1972,
PMID 4117815; Sjaastad 1976, PMID 1278191), infusion tests (Bozzao 1974, PMID
4408072), psychometric profiles (Rivero Hernández 1975, PMID 1211270) and shunt
series with "disappointing results" (Bannister 1972, PMID 4540544; Singounas
1976, PMID 1250499). Symon's demonstration that these patients have
intermittently *raised* pressure is a reason the term fell out of use for NPH.

These papers are relevant to the modern entity in two ways only: they show that
ventricular enlargement can progress without sustained hypertension, and they
are the source of persistent terminological confusion (e.g. a 2013 case report
still describing "acute normal pressure hydrocephalus" for what is clearly
post-LP low-pressure disease; Preuß 2013, PMID 23715811). The Kuba 2002 gait
study (PMID 11749014) and several 1970s "LPH" reports were retained in the core
axis because their abstracts describe low measured pressure with progressive
ventriculomegaly, but they should be read as NPH-era work.

## 2. Precursors in shunted patients (1974–1993)

- **McCullough & Fox 1974** (PMID 4813717): negative ICP in shunted adults and
  its relation to subdural haematoma — the first systematic recognition that
  shunted brains can run at sub-atmospheric pressure, here framed as an
  overdrainage complication rather than a hydrocephalic state.
- **McCullough 1986** (PMID 3491341): *symptomatic progressive ventriculomegaly in
  hydrocephalics with patent shunts and antisiphon devices* — the essential
  paradox (large ventricles, working shunt, low pressure) stated a decade before
  it was named. The anti-siphon device prevented the shunt from ever reaching
  the sub-zero pressures the brain needed.
- **Foltz & Blanks 1988** (PMID 3343612): *symptomatic low intracranial pressure in
  shunted hydrocephalus* — upright, activity-related symptoms with markedly low
  upright ICP, relieved by recumbency and Trendelenburg; Foltz later designed a
  "zero-ICP" gravity-controlled shunt (ZIPS; Foltz 1994, PMID 8194062).
- **Drake 1993** (PMID 8421544): functional obstruction of an anti-siphon device by
  tissue-capsule pressure — an early mechanical explanation for "patent shunt,
  large ventricles".
- **Pomeranz 1993** (PMID 8333302): feline intracranial hypotension reduces cerebral
  blood flow — an experimental hint that low ICP itself can be harmful.

## 3. Pang & Altschuler 1994 and the definitional decade

**Pang & Altschuler, Neurosurgery 1994** (PMID 7808607) coined *low-pressure
hydrocephalic state*: 12 shunted patients with symptomatic ventriculomegaly, ICP
2.2–6.6 mm Hg, patent shunts, requiring prolonged EVD at sub-zero levels
(most for weeks), with symptoms tracking ventricular size rather than ICP. They
proposed altered brain viscoelasticity — a "softened" parenchyma that no longer
returns to shape under a normal transmantle gradient — and established sub-zero
drainage as the reference treatment. Their four criteria (symptoms of
hydrocephalus, ventriculomegaly, ICP ≤ 5 cm H₂O, improvement with sub-zero
drainage) are still used for inclusion (Foster 2016; Villalonga 2025).

## 4. Classification proposals

- **Wu 2019** (PMID 30611954) proposed classifying hydrocephalus by ventricular
  pressure — high, normal, low (< 70 mm H₂O) and negative (< 0) — arguing that
  existing schemes (Oi's multi-categorical classification, PMID 21928018;
  Raimondi's unifying theory, PMID 8194058; Rekate's 2008 definition based on
  points of obstruction) give no management guidance for the low-pressure group.
- **Chinese Neuroscience Society 2025 consensus** (PMID 40734409) defines LPH as
  persistent intraventricular pressure < 70 mm H₂O with progressive
  ventriculomegaly and neurological worsening, adds imaging criteria (effaced
  Sylvian cisterns and convexity SAS, fourth-ventricular "ball sign"), and names
  fourth-ventricular outlet obstruction and altered compliance as candidate
  mechanisms. It is the first formal guideline document on the entity.
- **Lin 2025** (PMID 40826122) places low-pressure hydrocephalus as the second most
  common subtype of "refractory hydrocephalus" after infectious hydrocephalus and
  stresses that management hinges on "identifying and addressing the sites
  receiving CSF".
- **Rekate 2019** "Low or Negative Pressure Hydrocephalus Demystified" (PMID
  31103762) and **Rekate 2021** commentary (PMID 34272587) restate the
  turgor/disconnection view and argue the syndrome is a predictable consequence
  of CSF pathway anatomy rather than a mystery.

## 5. Terminological problems that remain

1. **Threshold**: ≤ 5 cm H₂O (Pang, Keough, Datta), < 70 mm H₂O (Wu, Chinese
   consensus), ≤ 0 (NegPH) and "below valve opening pressure" (Filippidis) are
   all in use; a case at 6–8 cm H₂O with a patent shunt set at 10 is LPH to
   some authors and shunt under-drainage to others.
2. **Acute vs chronic**: ALPH/SILPAH (deterioration over hours–days after a
   precipitant) and cNegPH / ultra-low-pressure NPH (months of refractory
   ventriculomegaly) probably share physiology but differ in urgency and in the
   role of ETV.
3. **NPH overlap**: Funnell 2020 and Trinh 2013 describe shunted iNPH patients who
   need siphoning; Doshi 2022 (PMID 36600762) describes an NPH patient evolving
   into VLPH. Whether "low-pressure NPH" is the same disease as post-SAH ALPH is
   unsettled.
4. **Sinking-flap / paradoxical herniation overlap**: after craniectomy the
   same sub-atmospheric physiology produces sinking flap syndrome, paradoxical
   herniation and LPH, sometimes sequentially in one patient (Bateman 2026, PMID
   42355921; Salam 2025).
